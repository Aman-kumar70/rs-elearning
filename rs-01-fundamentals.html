What Is Remote Sensing?

Remote sensing means learning about an object without touching it — and it works because every object on Earth interacts with light in its own particular way. This chapter builds the physics you need before any of the rest of the course makes sense: what's actually flowing from the Sun to a sensor, and why the atmosphere gets in the way.

01.1 / The Idea

Obtaining information about something without touching it

What

Remote sensing is the science of gathering information about an object, area, or phenomenon through analysis of data acquired by a device that is not in physical contact with it. A camera, your eye, and a satellite sensor are all, technically, remote sensing instruments.

Why

Because physical access is often impossible, dangerous, expensive, or simply too slow. You can't walk every square kilometre of the Amazon to track deforestation, or send a person into a cyclone to map flood extent — but a satellite passes over the whole planet every few days.

How

A sensor measures electromagnetic radiation that has interacted with a surface — reflected sunlight, emitted heat, or a radar pulse bounced back. The pattern of that energy across wavelengths and space is converted into a digital image, which is what the rest of this course is about.

In the remote sensing context specifically, we mean: a sensor mounted on a platform (aircraft or satellite) records energy reflected or emitted from the Earth's surface, from a distance, and that recording is turned into imagery we can interpret.

Two Kinds of Remote Sensing

Passive sensors use an external energy source — usually the Sun. They only work when the source is illuminating the target, which is why optical satellites can't see through cloud or darkness. Active sensors (RADAR, LiDAR) carry their own energy source, send it out, and measure what bounces back. That means they work day or night and can penetrate cloud cover — the trade-off is a more complex sensor and different image characteristics (radar images "look" very different from optical ones).

01.2 / Electromagnetic Radiation

The signal that carries the information

Electromagnetic (EM) radiation travels as waves, described by wavelength (distance between wave peaks) and frequency (how many waves pass a point per second). These are inversely related — short wavelength means high frequency, and vice versa. Different wavelengths interact with matter differently, which is the entire reason remote sensing works: a leaf and a rooftop reflect the same visible light similarly, but wildly differently in near-infrared.

c = speed of light (3×10⁸ m/s), λ = wavelength, ν = frequency, E = energy of a photon, h = Planck's constant. Shorter wavelength → higher frequency → higher energy. This is why UV and X-rays are dangerous and radio waves are not.

Why the Atmosphere Matters

Not every wavelength makes it from the ground to a satellite. Gases like water vapour, CO₂, and ozone absorb specific wavelength bands almost completely. The wavelength ranges that do pass through relatively unobstructed are called atmospheric windows — and sensors are deliberately designed to operate inside them. This is why satellite bands cluster around 0.4–2.5 µm (optical/NIR/SWIR window), 8–14 µm (thermal window), and the microwave region (which passes through almost unimpeded, including through clouds).

Key Takeaways
  • Remote sensing = gathering information about an object without physical contact, by measuring the electromagnetic energy it reflects or emits.
  • Passive sensors depend on sunlight (optical cameras); active sensors (RADAR, LiDAR) provide their own energy and work day/night, through cloud.
  • Wavelength and frequency are inversely related (c = λν); higher frequency = higher photon energy (E = hν).
  • The EM spectrum used in remote sensing spans far beyond visible light: NIR, SWIR, thermal IR, and microwave each reveal different surface properties.
  • The atmosphere blocks most wavelengths — sensors are built to operate inside the atmospheric windows that do get through.
