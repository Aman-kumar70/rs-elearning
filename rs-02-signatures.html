Spectral Signatures

Every material on Earth has a light "fingerprint" — the unique way it reflects and absorbs energy across wavelengths. Learn to read these curves and you can tell a satellite image what it's looking at, without ever visiting the place.

02.1 / The Concept

A "fingerprint" made of light

What

A spectral signature (or spectral reflectance curve) is a plot of a material's reflectance (%) against wavelength. Vegetation, water, soil and snow each trace a distinctive curve — that shape is the identity of the material.

Why

Because it's how automated classification works. A computer can't "see" a forest the way you do — but it can compare a pixel's reflectance pattern across bands to known signatures and decide "this pixel is vegetation" with no human ever looking at the image.

How

Multispectral sensors record reflectance in several discrete bands (blue, green, red, NIR, SWIR...). Plotting the DN value of a pixel across those bands approximates its spectral curve — the more bands, the more precise the fingerprint (hyperspectral sensors use hundreds).

02.2 / Vegetation

Why healthy plants glow in the infrared

A leaf's cell structure — spongy mesophyll tissue full of air pockets — scatters near-infrared light back out extremely efficiently, while chlorophyll absorbs blue and red light for photosynthesis. Stressed, dying, or sparse vegetation loses this NIR reflectance dramatically before it ever turns visibly yellow or brown — which is why NIR-based indices can detect crop stress before a farmer walking the field would notice it.

NDVI — Turning the Curve Into One Number

The steep rise from red to NIR is so diagnostic that it's been condensed into a single index used almost everywhere in agriculture and ecology: the Normalized Difference Vegetation Index.

NDVI = (NIR − Red) / (NIR + Red)
Ranges from −1 to +1. Dense healthy vegetation: 0.6–0.9. Sparse/stressed vegetation: 0.2–0.5. Bare soil: near 0. Water: negative. You'll compute this yourself with real satellite data in the Image Processing track.
plot_signature.py
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# Approximate reflectance (%) at 5 representative wavelengths (µm)
wavelengths = [0.48, 0.56, 0.66, 0.86, 1.65]   # Blue, Green, Red, NIR, SWIR
vegetation  = [4, 9, 5, 45, 22]
dry_soil    = [16, 20, 24, 32, 38]
water       = [7, 5, 3, 1, 0.5]

plt.plot(wavelengths, vegetation, 'o-', color='green',  label='Vegetation')
plt.plot(wavelengths, dry_soil,   'o-', color='saddlebrown', label='Dry soil')
plt.plot(wavelengths, water,      'o-', color='blue',  label='Water')
plt.xlabel('Wavelength (µm)'); plt.ylabel('Reflectance (%)')
plt.legend(); plt.title('Spectral Reflectance Curves')
plt.savefig('signatures.png', dpi=150)
02.3 / Soil, Water & Snow

The other three signatures you'll see constantly

🟤

Soil

Soil's curve is smoother than vegetation's — no sharp chlorophyll dips. Five properties control it, in order of importance: moisture (wet soil is darker/lower reflectance across all wavelengths), organic content, structure/texture, iron oxide content (raises reflectance in the red, lowers it in green), and surface roughness.

💧

Water

Water absorbs NIR and SWIR almost completely — reflectance beyond ~0.9 µm is close to zero. This is actually useful: NIR bands are the easiest way to draw a clean water/land boundary. In the visible range, clear water reflects a little blue-green; turbid (sediment-loaded) water reflects more; algae-rich water shows a green reflectance peak from chlorophyll.

❄️

Snow

Fresh snow has very high visible reflectance (up to 80%) but drops off in SWIR. Grain size (which increases with age), liquid water content (melting snow reflects less), and contamination (soot, dust) all shift the curve — so SWIR bands are used to distinguish old snow from fresh snow, not just visible brightness.

Try It Yourself

Pick any two materials from the interactive chart above and predict which single band would separate them most cleanly — i.e. where the vertical gap between their curves is largest. That's exactly the logic band ratios and vegetation indices are built on (see IP.3, Enhancement).

Key Takeaways
  • Every material has a distinctive spectral reflectance curve — its identity in light.
  • Vegetation: low in blue/red (chlorophyll absorption), small green peak, sharp NIR jump. Stress shows up as an NIR drop before visible colour change.
  • NDVI = (NIR − Red)/(NIR + Red) condenses the vegetation signature into one comparable number.
  • Soil reflectance is dominated by moisture content; water absorbs almost all NIR/SWIR; snow is bright in visible but distinguishable by age in SWIR.
  • Classification algorithms (Track B, IP.4) work by comparing a pixel's measured signature to known reference signatures.
